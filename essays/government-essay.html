Government WAEC 2024

Q1. Define federalism and discuss FOUR features of a federal system of government.

Marks: 20 — Definition (4 marks) + Four features (4 × 4 = 16 marks)

📝 Model Answer

Definition: Federalism is a system of government in which constitutional powers are divided between a central (national) authority and component (state or regional) units, with each level deriving its authority directly from the constitution and being supreme within its own sphere of jurisdiction. Examples include Nigeria, the United States, India, Canada, and Germany.

K.C. Wheare, the foremost authority on federalism, defined it as "a method of dividing powers so that general and regional governments are each, within a sphere, coordinate and independent."

Four Features of a Federal System:

1. Written and Rigid Constitution: A federal system requires a written constitution that clearly outlines the powers of the central government and component units. The constitution must be rigid (difficult to amend) to protect the autonomy of states. The Nigerian 1999 Constitution and the U.S. Constitution exemplify this — neither can be amended without the consent of a substantial proportion of state legislatures.

2. Division of Powers: Powers are constitutionally distributed between the federal and state governments through three lists: the Exclusive List (federal only — defence, foreign affairs, currency), the Concurrent List (both levels — education, health), and the Residual List (states only — chieftaincy, local matters). This prevents domination by either level.

3. Supremacy of the Constitution: The constitution is the supreme law of the land. No law made by any level of government may contradict it. The judiciary, particularly the Supreme Court, interprets the constitution and resolves disputes between federal and state governments. This judicial supremacy ensures constitutional integrity.

4. Bicameral Legislature: Federal systems typically operate two legislative chambers. The lower house represents the population (Nigeria's House of Representatives, U.S. House of Representatives), while the upper house represents the component units equally (Nigeria's Senate gives each state 3 senators; U.S. Senate gives each state 2). This balances majority rule with state representation.

💡 Other features you could mention: Independent judiciary, dual citizenship (in some federations), revenue sharing formula, separate civil services, residual powers reserved for states.

📋 WAEC Marking Guide

  • Definition: Clear, accurate, references authority (Wheare) — 4 marks
  • Each feature: Name (1) + Explanation (2) + Example (1) = 4 marks
  • Logical organisation, well-developed paragraphs
  • Clear examples (Nigeria, USA) for credibility
Government WAEC 2023

Q2. Explain the doctrine of separation of powers and state THREE advantages.

📝 Model Answer

Definition: The doctrine of separation of powers is the constitutional principle that divides governmental authority into three distinct branches — the Executive, the Legislature, and the Judiciary — each with separate functions, personnel, and powers. The doctrine was popularised by the French philosopher Baron de Montesquieu in his 1748 work The Spirit of the Laws, building on earlier ideas by John Locke.

Under this doctrine: the Legislature makes laws (Nigerian National Assembly, U.S. Congress, U.K. Parliament); the Executive implements and enforces laws (the President, Cabinet, civil service); and the Judiciary interprets laws and resolves disputes (Supreme Court, Court of Appeal, lower courts).

While complete separation is impractical, modern democracies practice separation of powers alongside checks and balances, where each arm has limited powers to oversee the others — for example, the legislature may impeach the executive, the executive may veto legislation, and the judiciary may declare laws unconstitutional.

Three Advantages of Separation of Powers:

1. Prevention of Tyranny and Abuse of Power: By dividing governmental authority among three independent organs, no single person or group can monopolise power. Concentration of power in one body, as Montesquieu warned, leads inevitably to despotism. The fall of dictators throughout history — from absolute monarchs to military rulers — demonstrates the dangers of unified power. Separation ensures that ambition checks ambition.

2. Promotion of Efficiency and Specialisation: Each branch focuses on its core function, allowing personnel to develop expertise. Legislators specialise in policy debate; judges in legal interpretation; executives in administration. This specialisation produces better governance than a system where one body attempts all functions.

3. Protection of Individual Liberty and Rule of Law: When the judiciary is independent of the executive, citizens can challenge unlawful government actions in court without fear. The judiciary protects fundamental human rights against legislative overreach and executive abuse. Notable examples include the U.S. Supreme Court's Brown v. Board of Education (1954) and the Nigerian Supreme Court's many checks on military and civilian governments.

📋 WAEC Marking Guide

  • Clear definition with reference to Montesquieu
  • Identification of all three arms with examples
  • Each advantage explained with supporting reasoning
  • Connection to checks and balances earns extra marks

Q3. Define democracy and discuss FOUR features of a democratic government.

📝 Model Answer

Definition: Democracy is a system of government in which sovereignty resides in the people, who exercise their power either directly or through elected representatives. The term originates from the Greek words "demos" (people) and "kratos" (rule), meaning "rule by the people." Abraham Lincoln, in his Gettysburg Address (1863), famously defined democracy as "government of the people, by the people, for the people."

Democracy may be direct (citizens vote on policies themselves, as in ancient Athens or modern Switzerland) or representative (citizens elect officials to govern on their behalf, as in Nigeria, the UK, and the USA).

Four Features of a Democratic Government:

1. Popular Sovereignty and Periodic Free Elections: Government derives its legitimacy from the consent of the governed, expressed through periodic elections. These elections must be free, fair, transparent, and competitive. Adult citizens enjoy universal suffrage — the right to vote regardless of religion, ethnicity, gender, or wealth. Nigeria conducts general elections every four years, allowing citizens to renew or change their government.

2. Rule of Law: All persons — including government officials — are equal before the law and bound by it. No one is above the law. The constitution and statutes apply equally to the president and the ordinary citizen. This principle, championed by A.V. Dicey, ensures predictability, justice, and the protection of rights.

3. Fundamental Human Rights and Civil Liberties: Democratic governments guarantee citizens basic freedoms — freedom of speech, religion, assembly, association, movement, and the press. These rights are typically enshrined in the constitution. Chapter IV of the Nigerian Constitution explicitly protects these rights, and citizens may approach the courts for redress when these rights are violated.

4. Independent Judiciary and Separation of Powers: Democracy requires that the judiciary be independent of executive and legislative interference. Judges should be appointed on merit, secure in tenure, and free to interpret the law without political pressure. Combined with the principle of separation of powers, this prevents tyranny and protects democratic institutions.

💡 Bonus features: Multi-party system, free press, civic education, peaceful transfer of power, accountability of officials, decentralisation of power.
Government WAEC 2021

Q4. Describe the pre-colonial political system of the Yoruba people.

📝 Model Answer

The pre-colonial Yoruba political system, particularly that of the Old Oyo Empire (which flourished between the 14th and 19th centuries), was a sophisticated and well-organised structure that combined monarchy with checks and balances. It is one of Africa's most studied indigenous political systems and is celebrated for its constitutional limitations on royal power.

1. The Alaafin (Oba)

At the apex was the Alaafin, the supreme political and spiritual ruler of the empire. The Alaafin was selected from among the male descendants of the founding ruler, Oranmiyan, by the Oyo Mesi. He resided in the Afin (palace) at Oyo-Ile and was regarded as a divine king (Ekeji Orisa — "second to the gods"). His person was sacred; commoners were forbidden from looking directly at him. He commanded the army, presided over religious ceremonies, and served as the highest court of appeal.

2. The Oyo Mesi (Council of Seven)

The Oyo Mesi were a council of seven hereditary kingmakers headed by the Bashorun (Prime Minister). Other members included the Agbakin, Samu, Alapini, Laguna, Akiniku, and Asipa. They selected new Alaafins, advised the king on matters of state, and crucially, served as a check on royal power. If an Alaafin became tyrannical, the Bashorun could present him with an empty calabash (or parrot's egg), symbolically requesting his abdication or suicide.

3. The Ogboni Society

The Ogboni was a powerful cult of elders that served as a religious and judicial body. They worshipped the earth goddess (Ile) and adjudicated serious disputes, including those between the Alaafin and the Oyo Mesi. The Ogboni's role provided a vital balance — they could overrule both the king and the kingmakers, ensuring that no single body abused its authority.

4. The Are-Ona-Kakanfo

The Are-Ona-Kakanfo was the supreme military commander, appointed by the Alaafin to lead the empire's armies in major wars. He was forbidden from residing in Oyo-Ile to prevent him from threatening the throne. Failure in war was punishable by ritual suicide. Famous Kakanfos include Kurunmi and Latoosa.

5. Provincial Administration (Ajele System)

The empire was divided into provinces ruled by subordinate Obas (such as the Onipopo of Popo and the Olowu of Owu). The Alaafin appointed Ajele (royal commissioners) to these provinces to collect tribute and ensure loyalty. The Ilari (palace messengers) carried royal commands across the empire.

Conclusion: The Yoruba political system was thus characterised by an intricate balance of executive (Alaafin), advisory (Oyo Mesi), judicial-religious (Ogboni), and military (Kakanfo) institutions. This structure provided remarkable political stability for centuries until internal divisions and external pressures led to the empire's decline in the 19th century.

📋 WAEC Marking Guide

  • Identification of major institutions (Alaafin, Oyo Mesi, Ogboni, Kakanfo) — 4 marks each
  • Functions clearly explained
  • Mention of checks and balances earns bonus marks
  • Specific details (calabash tradition, Bashorun) demonstrate depth

Q5. What is a pressure group? Distinguish between pressure groups and political parties.

📝 Model Answer

Definition: A pressure group (also called an interest group or lobby group) is an organised body of people with shared interests or objectives who seek to influence government policy without themselves seeking to form a government. Examples in Nigeria include the Nigeria Labour Congress (NLC), Nigerian Medical Association (NMA), Manufacturers Association of Nigeria (MAN), and the Academic Staff Union of Universities (ASUU).

Pressure groups operate by lobbying lawmakers, sponsoring research, organising rallies, conducting strikes, publishing position papers, and engaging the media to draw attention to their causes.

Differences Between Pressure Groups and Political Parties

1. Aim/Objective

Political parties aim to win political power and form government through elections. The All Progressives Congress (APC) and the Peoples Democratic Party (PDP) contest elections to control the executive and legislature. Pressure groups, however, do not seek to govern; they seek only to influence those in power to advance their members' interests.

2. Membership

Political parties have broad, open membership across all professions, ethnicities, and classes. Anyone who agrees with the party's manifesto may join. Pressure groups typically have narrow, specialised membership tied to a profession or specific cause — only doctors join NMA, only lawyers join NBA.

3. Scope of Issues

Political parties address a wide range of national issues — economy, security, foreign policy, education — through their manifestos. Pressure groups focus on narrow, specific issues directly affecting their members (e.g., ASUU focuses primarily on university education and lecturers' welfare).

4. Methods

Political parties contest elections, run campaigns, and form governments. Pressure groups use lobbying, strikes, demonstrations, petitions, and media campaigns — methods that do not involve seeking elective office.

5. Public Accountability

Political parties in government are directly accountable to the electorate at the next election. Pressure groups are accountable only to their members, not to the general public.

💡 Types of pressure groups: Sectional groups (defend members' interests — NLC, NMA), Promotional/Cause groups (advocate for general public good — Amnesty International, BudgIT, anti-corruption groups).

Q6. State and explain FIVE sources of the constitution.

📝 Model Answer

A constitution is the supreme body of fundamental rules and principles that govern a state, defining the powers and limits of government and the rights and duties of citizens. Constitutions are derived from various sources, both written and unwritten:

1. Custom and Tradition: Long-established practices and traditional ways of life form a vital source. Many constitutions, particularly in Britain, evolved from customs accepted over centuries — such as the convention that the monarch must give royal assent to bills passed by Parliament. In Nigeria, traditional rulers' roles in some constitutional arrangements draw from custom.

2. Judicial Precedents (Case Law): Decisions of superior courts on constitutional matters become binding sources of constitutional law. The doctrine of stare decisis ensures consistency. Landmark cases like Marbury v. Madison (USA, 1803) — which established judicial review — and Nigerian Supreme Court rulings on federalism shape constitutional understanding.

3. Conventions: These are unwritten rules of political practice that, although not legally enforceable, are religiously observed by political actors. For instance, the convention that Nigeria's presidency rotates between the North and South, or that ministers should be appointed from each state.

4. Acts of Parliament/Legislative Enactments: Statutes passed by the legislature can amend or supplement the constitution. The British Parliament's Representation of the People Acts and Nigeria's various Constitutional Amendment Acts (e.g., the Fourth Alteration Act of 2017) illustrate this source.

5. Historical Documents and Treaties: Foundational charters and international treaties contribute to constitutional law. Britain's Magna Carta (1215), Bill of Rights (1689), and the Act of Union (1707) form parts of its unwritten constitution. Nigeria's Independence Constitution (1960) and Republican Constitution (1963) drew from prior colonial Orders-in-Council.

6. Opinions of Constitutional Experts (Academic Writings): Scholarly works by jurists like A.V. Dicey, Sir Ivor Jennings, and Nigerian constitutional scholars (Ben Nwabueze, Itse Sagay) are frequently cited and considered influential sources, especially when courts interpret constitutional provisions.

Government WAEC 2024

Q7. Outline FIVE functions of the legislature in a democratic state.

📝 Model Answer

The legislature, also known as parliament or congress, is the law-making organ of government. In Nigeria, the legislature comprises the National Assembly (Senate and House of Representatives) at the federal level, and the State Houses of Assembly at the state level. Its functions are critical to democratic governance:

1. Law-Making (Primary Function): The legislature's most important function is to make laws for the peace, order, and good government of the state. Bills pass through First Reading, Second Reading, Committee Stage, Report Stage, and Third Reading before being sent to the executive for assent. Examples include the Nigerian Petroleum Industry Act (2021) and the Companies and Allied Matters Act (CAMA, 2020).

2. Control of Public Finance (Power of the Purse): No public money may be spent without legislative approval. The legislature debates, amends, and passes the Annual Appropriation Bill (Budget) presented by the executive. It also scrutinises government expenditure through the Public Accounts Committee, ensuring accountability and preventing financial recklessness.

3. Oversight of the Executive: The legislature holds the executive accountable through investigations, hearings, and committee work. Ministers and heads of agencies are summoned to explain their actions. The Senate confirms key executive appointments (ministers, ambassadors, judges of the Supreme Court). In extreme cases, the legislature may impeach the President for gross misconduct.

4. Constitutional Amendment: The legislature plays a central role in amending the constitution. In Nigeria, constitutional amendments require approval by two-thirds of the National Assembly and ratification by at least 24 of the 36 State Houses of Assembly. This makes the legislature the custodian of constitutional change.

5. Representation of the People: Members of the legislature represent their constituencies, conveying citizens' concerns to the seat of power and explaining government policies back to the people. They make laws reflecting their constituents' needs and address local development issues through constituency projects.

6. Ratification of International Treaties: Treaties and international agreements signed by the executive must be ratified by the legislature before they become binding domestic law. This ensures that Nigeria's foreign commitments enjoy democratic legitimacy.

Government WAEC 2023

Q8. Discuss the fundamental rights and duties of a citizen.

📝 Model Answer

Citizenship is the legal relationship between an individual and the state, conferring certain rights and imposing certain duties. In Nigeria, citizenship is acquired by birth, registration, or naturalisation, as outlined in Chapter III of the 1999 Constitution.

Fundamental Rights of a Citizen

These are basic rights guaranteed by the constitution and enforceable in court. Chapter IV of the Nigerian Constitution lists them:

1. Right to Life (Section 33): Every person has the right to life, and no one shall be deprived of it intentionally except in execution of a court sentence in respect of a criminal offence.

2. Right to Dignity of the Human Person (Section 34): No person shall be subjected to torture, inhuman, or degrading treatment, or held in slavery or servitude.

3. Right to Personal Liberty (Section 35): Every person is entitled to personal liberty, except in lawful detention pursuant to a court order or for purposes prescribed by law.

4. Right to Fair Hearing (Section 36): Every person is entitled to a fair, public, and impartial hearing within a reasonable time before an independent tribunal.

5. Right to Privacy (Section 37): The privacy of citizens, their homes, correspondence, and telephone conversations is guaranteed and protected.

6. Right to Freedom of Thought, Conscience, and Religion (Section 38): Citizens may freely profess and practice any religion or change their religion without coercion.

7. Right to Freedom of Expression and the Press (Section 39): Citizens may hold opinions and impart ideas without interference, and may own and operate media outlets.

8. Right to Peaceful Assembly and Association (Section 40): Citizens may form or belong to any political party, trade union, or association.

9. Right to Freedom of Movement (Section 41): Citizens may move freely throughout Nigeria and reside in any part.

10. Right to Freedom from Discrimination (Section 42): No citizen shall be discriminated against on grounds of ethnicity, religion, sex, or political opinion.

Duties of a Citizen

Rights come with corresponding duties:

1. Obedience to the Constitution and Laws: Every citizen must abide by the laws of the land.

2. Payment of Taxes: Citizens must pay all taxes due — personal income tax, VAT, customs duties — to fund public services.

3. Defence of the Country: Citizens must defend Nigeria when called upon, including service in the armed forces during national emergencies.

4. Participation in Civic Activities: Citizens should participate in elections by voting, hold leaders accountable, and contribute to community development.

5. Respect for the National Symbols: Citizens must respect the national flag, national anthem, coat of arms, and constituted authority.

6. Protection of Public Property: Citizens must safeguard public property and the environment, refraining from vandalism and pollution.

7. Reporting Crimes and Cooperation with Law Enforcement: Citizens have a duty to report crimes and assist security agencies in maintaining law and order.

Q9. Define public opinion and outline FOUR ways of measuring it.

📝 Model Answer

Definition: Public opinion refers to the collective views, attitudes, and beliefs held by the majority of the people in a society on issues of public concern at a particular time. It is a powerful force in democratic governance, capable of influencing policy, shaping elections, and toppling governments. Lord Bryce defined it as "the aggregate of the views men hold regarding matters that affect or interest the community."

Public opinion is dynamic — it changes with events, information, and circumstances — and is influenced by the media, religious institutions, education, family, peer groups, and political parties.

Four Ways of Measuring Public Opinion:

1. Opinion Polls and Surveys: Specialised research organisations (such as NOIPolls in Nigeria, Gallup internationally, Afrobarometer in Africa) conduct scientifically designed surveys with representative samples of the population. Respondents are asked structured questions, and statistical analysis produces estimates of public sentiment on issues like presidential approval, election preferences, or policy support. Modern polls have margins of error and confidence levels.

2. Elections and Referenda: The most authoritative measure of public opinion is the ballot box. Periodic elections allow citizens to express their views on parties, candidates, and policies. Referenda — direct votes on specific issues (such as the 2016 Brexit referendum in the UK) — provide unambiguous measurements of opinion on single questions.

3. The Media (Press, Radio, Television, Social Media): Newspapers, broadcast media, and especially social media platforms (Twitter/X, Facebook, Instagram) reflect and shape public opinion. Trending topics, editorial positions, talk-show debates, listener call-ins, and online comments collectively reveal the public's mood. The volume and tone of coverage indicate which issues matter most.

4. Public Demonstrations, Protests, and Petitions: Mass protests, peaceful marches, strikes, and online petitions visibly demonstrate strong public feeling. Examples include Nigeria's #EndSARS protests of October 2020, which forced the government to disband the SARS unit, or the Arab Spring uprisings of 2011. Similarly, large-scale petitions delivered to government signal collective concerns.

5. Pressure Groups' Activities: The lobbying, position papers, press releases, and rallies of organised pressure groups (NLC, NBA, civil society) reflect specific segments of public opinion.

6. Letters to Editors and Phone-In Programmes: Citizens express opinions through letters to newspapers and call-in radio/TV programmes, providing qualitative insights into public thinking.

Government WAEC 2021

Q10. Discuss the objectives and achievements of ECOWAS.

📝 Model Answer

The Economic Community of West African States (ECOWAS) is a regional intergovernmental organisation established by the Treaty of Lagos on 28th May, 1975. Founded under the leadership of General Yakubu Gowon of Nigeria and President Gnassingbé Eyadéma of Togo, ECOWAS comprises 15 member states (currently: Benin, Burkina Faso, Cape Verde, Côte d'Ivoire, The Gambia, Ghana, Guinea, Guinea-Bissau, Liberia, Mali, Niger, Nigeria, Senegal, Sierra Leone, and Togo).

Objectives of ECOWAS

1. Economic Integration: To promote cooperation and integration leading to the establishment of an economic union in West Africa, raising the standard of living of the people through coordinated economic policies.

2. Free Movement of Persons, Goods, and Services: To remove barriers to the movement of citizens, goods, capital, and services among member states. This includes the abolition of customs duties between members.

3. Common External Tariff: To establish a common external tariff and trade policy with non-member states.

4. Harmonisation of Economic Policies: To harmonise agricultural, industrial, fiscal, monetary, and infrastructural policies among member states.

5. Promotion of Peace, Security, and Democracy: Originally focused on economic matters, ECOWAS expanded its mandate (revised treaty of 1993) to include conflict prevention, peacekeeping, and the promotion of democratic governance.

Achievements of ECOWAS

1. Free Movement of Persons: Through the ECOWAS passport and travel certificates, citizens of member states can move freely across borders without visa requirements for stays of up to 90 days. This has dramatically increased trade, tourism, and cultural exchange.

2. Establishment of ECOWAS Monitoring Group (ECOMOG): ECOMOG, the regional peacekeeping force, has played decisive roles in restoring peace in Liberia (1990s), Sierra Leone (1997–2000), Guinea-Bissau (1998), and The Gambia (2017, when it removed Yahya Jammeh after he refused to step down). This pioneered African solutions to African problems.

3. ECOWAS Trade Liberalisation Scheme (ETLS): Goods produced within the region enjoy duty-free movement, boosting intra-regional trade.

4. Promotion of Democratic Governance: The ECOWAS Protocol on Democracy and Good Governance (2001) prohibits unconstitutional changes of government. ECOWAS has imposed sanctions on coup-affected states (Mali 2020, Guinea 2021, Burkina Faso 2022, Niger 2023), demonstrating commitment to democratic norms.

5. ECOWAS Court of Justice: Established in 2001 in Abuja, this court adjudicates disputes among member states and protects the human rights of citizens of the community.

6. Common Currency Project (ECO): Although not yet fully implemented, work continues on the proposed common currency (ECO) to facilitate further integration, similar to the Euro in Europe.

7. Infrastructure Development: ECOWAS has facilitated cross-border road projects (Trans-West African Coastal Highway), the West African Power Pool, and the West African Gas Pipeline.

Conclusion: Despite challenges — including political instability in some member states, economic disparities, and recent withdrawals threatened by Mali, Burkina Faso, and Niger — ECOWAS has remained one of Africa's most effective regional organisations, advancing both economic integration and democratic stability in West Africa.
