Essay Writing Tips for WAEC

  • Structure matters: Introduction (10%), Body (75%), Conclusion (15%) — typically 4–5 paragraphs total
  • Word count: Aim for 450–600 words. Too short loses content marks; too long risks irrelevance
  • Use connectives: Furthermore, However, In addition, Consequently, In conclusion
  • Vocabulary: Demonstrate range with appropriate, precise word choice — avoid repetition
  • Grammar: Vary sentence structures; check tense consistency, subject-verb agreement, punctuation
  • Plan first: Spend 5 minutes outlining your 3-4 main points before writing
English Argumentative WAEC 2023

Essay 1: Argumentative Essay

Question: "Boarding schools are better than day schools." Write FOR or AGAINST the motion.

📝 Model Answer (FOR the motion)

Mr. Chairman, panel of judges, accurate timekeeper, co-debaters, ladies and gentlemen. I stand before you today to support the motion that boarding schools are better than day schools. While my opponents may argue that day schools provide the comfort of home, I shall demonstrate that the benefits of boarding education far outweigh any perceived shortcomings.

To begin with, boarding schools cultivate self-reliance and independence in students. Living away from parental supervision, students must learn to manage their time, finances, and personal hygiene without constant prompting. This early exposure to responsibility prepares them for the realities of adult life, including university studies and the workplace. Day school students, by contrast, often remain dependent on parental care well into their teenage years.

Furthermore, boarding schools offer a structured academic environment that maximises learning opportunities. With designated prep periods, evening study sessions, and immediate access to teachers, boarding students enjoy a study schedule that is virtually impossible to replicate at home. Distractions such as television, social media, and household chores are systematically minimised, allowing students to focus entirely on their academic and co-curricular pursuits.

In addition, boarding schools provide a richer social experience. Students from diverse backgrounds — different ethnic groups, religions, and socio-economic classes — live, study, and play together. This exposure builds tolerance, communication skills, and lifelong friendships. The social isolation common among day students who return home immediately after lessons is wholly absent in boarding schools.

However, my opponents may argue that boarding schools are expensive and emotionally draining. While there is truth in the cost, the long-term benefits — academic excellence, character formation, and networking — represent an invaluable investment. As for emotional adjustment, this builds resilience, a quality essential for success in modern life.

In conclusion, boarding schools, with their emphasis on independence, structured learning, and diverse social environments, are indisputably superior to day schools. I therefore urge this august audience to support the motion. Thank you.

📋 WAEC Marking Guide

  • Content (10 marks): 3-4 well-developed arguments, balanced rebuttal
  • Organisation (10 marks): Clear introduction, logical body, strong conclusion
  • Expression (20 marks): Varied sentence structure, formal register, accurate grammar
  • Mechanical Accuracy (10 marks): Correct spelling, punctuation, paragraphing
  • Total: 50 marks
English Narrative WAEC 2022

Essay 2: Narrative Essay

Question: Write a story that ends with: "...and that was how I learnt the most important lesson of my life."

📝 Model Answer

The harmattan winds of December had wrapped Lagos in their dusty embrace when my grandmother fell seriously ill. I was sixteen, a notorious daydreamer, and the kind of grandson who promised to visit but rarely did. My final WAEC exams loomed, and books had become my sole excuse for every neglected duty.

"Tunde, please come and see me," she pleaded over the crackling phone line one evening. "I have something important to tell you." I muttered something about Mathematics revision and quickly ended the call. Three weeks passed. Then four. The phone rang one Friday afternoon, and my mother's trembling voice told me what my heart had refused to anticipate. Grandmother was gone.

The journey to Ijebu-Ode for the funeral was the longest of my life. Each kilometre seemed to mock my procrastination, each tree by the roadside whispering accusations. When we arrived, my Aunt Bola met me at the door with red-rimmed eyes and an envelope in her hands. "She wrote this for you," she said quietly. "She kept asking when you would come."

That night, alone in the room where Grandmother had spent her final days, I unfolded the letter. Her familiar, looped handwriting filled three pages. She wrote of her childhood during the war, the sacrifices my mother had made, and the dreams she had for me. She forgave me for not visiting. "Life," she wrote, "is shorter than you think. Love the people who love you, and tell them often. Books will wait, but people will not."

I wept until dawn — for her gentle wisdom, for my selfish neglect, for every moment I had taken for granted. The next morning, I called every relative I had been ignoring. I visited my elderly father's brother, who lived alone in Mushin. I sat with my younger cousins and listened, truly listened, to their childish stories.

Today, ten years later, I keep that letter framed on my desk. Whenever ambition tempts me to neglect those who matter, I read her words again. ...and that was how I learnt the most important lesson of my life.

📋 WAEC Marking Guide

  • Plot: Clear beginning, conflict, climax, resolution
  • Characterisation: Believable narrator, vivid supporting characters
  • Setting: Specific place/time references (Lagos, Ijebu-Ode, harmattan)
  • Theme: Loss, regret, family — moral integrated naturally
  • Style: Sensory details, dialogue, emotional authenticity
  • Mandatory ending: Story must close with the given line ✓
English Descriptive WAEC 2021

Essay 3: Descriptive Essay

Question: Describe a typical market day in your community.

📝 Model Answer

Long before the rooster's first crow, the Oja Oba market in Ilorin begins to stir. By 5 a.m., the dirt road leading to the market is already a river of human bodies, head pans balanced impossibly atop wrappered heads, wooden wheelbarrows squeaking under mountains of yam tubers, and the occasional sleepy donkey laden with sacks of guinea corn from the northern villages.

By 7 a.m., the market has fully awakened. The vegetable section, my favourite, is a riot of colour: emerald-green ugu leaves bound with twisted palm fronds, scarlet tomatoes piled in geometric perfection, golden mounds of cured maize, and the deep purple of garden eggs glistening in the morning light. Mama Risikat, who has sold spices here for thirty years, beckons customers with practiced charm: "Come, my daughter — the freshest pepper in Ilorin, just for you!"

The sounds are equally enchanting. The rhythmic chop-chop of cleavers slicing through goat meat at the butchers' corner. The metallic clang of the blacksmith hammering hoe blades. Children's laughter rising above the din as they chase each other between stalls. And everywhere, the constant negotiation: "Two hundred naira!" "No, I beg, take one fifty!" — the music of commerce that has played in African markets for centuries.

The smells are intoxicating, sometimes overwhelming. The sharp, smoky aroma of roasted plantain mingles with the earthy scent of fresh yam, the pungent fragrance of dried fish, and the sweet perfume of ripening pineapples. From the food section drift the comforting smells of amala being pounded and ewedu soup simmering in clay pots, drawing hungry traders to a quick mid-morning meal.

By midday, the heat is fierce, but business never slows. Tailors hunched over old Singer machines stitch ankara dresses while customers wait. Bookshop owners arrange textbooks in neat stacks. Money-changers count crisp naira notes with miraculous speed. Children weave through the crowd selling sachets of pure water, their voices clear above the bustle: "Pure water! Cold pure water!"

As the sun finally surrenders to the horizon, the market begins its slow exhalation. Stalls are dismantled, unsold goods packed away, and the dust of a thousand transactions settles. Yet tomorrow, before dawn, the cycle will begin again — the timeless heartbeat of my community.

📋 WAEC Marking Guide

  • Sensory details: Sight, sound, smell, taste, touch all present ✓
  • Vivid imagery: "river of human bodies", "riot of colour", figurative language
  • Specific examples: Named persons (Mama Risikat), specific items (ugu, ankara)
  • Organisation: Logical flow — early morning → midday → evening
  • Cultural authenticity: Local items, dialogue, customs naturally woven in
English Expository WAEC 2020

Essay 4: Expository Essay

Question: Discuss the causes and effects of unemployment among Nigerian graduates.

📝 Model Answer

Graduate unemployment in Nigeria has become one of the most pressing socio-economic challenges of the twenty-first century. According to the National Bureau of Statistics, over 33 percent of Nigerian graduates remain unemployed years after completing their first degree. This essay examines the principal causes of this crisis and its devastating effects on individuals, families, and the nation at large.

The first major cause is the mismatch between university curricula and industry requirements. Many Nigerian universities continue to teach theoretical content that bears little relation to current job market needs. Graduates emerge with degrees but lack practical skills in digital literacy, data analysis, communication, and problem-solving — competencies that employers urgently demand. Consequently, even highly qualified graduates struggle to secure employment.

Secondly, the slow pace of economic growth and industrialisation has dramatically reduced the rate of job creation. While Nigerian universities produce approximately 600,000 graduates annually, the formal economy creates fewer than 100,000 new jobs in the same period. The collapse of major industries, dependency on oil exports, and discouragement of manufacturing have shrunk the labour market considerably.

Thirdly, corruption and nepotism in recruitment exclude qualified but unconnected candidates. Many positions in both the public and private sectors are reserved for relatives of influential figures, leaving graduates without political connections perpetually unemployed regardless of merit.

The effects of this widespread unemployment are equally disturbing. On the individual level, prolonged joblessness leads to depression, low self-esteem, and in extreme cases, suicide. Many graduates relocate abroad through dangerous routes, contributing to the brain drain crisis. On the family level, parents who invested heavily in their children's education face financial ruin and emotional disappointment.

On the national level, graduate unemployment fuels insecurity. Unemployed youth become easy recruits for armed robbery, kidnapping, internet fraud (Yahoo-Yahoo), and political thuggery. The economy also loses billions in potential productivity, while the government bears the burden of social welfare for the jobless.

In conclusion, the causes of graduate unemployment in Nigeria are complex and interconnected. Addressing this challenge requires curriculum reform, economic diversification, transparent recruitment, and significant investment in entrepreneurship education. Until these measures are implemented, the nation's most educated minds will continue to languish in idleness, undermining Nigeria's developmental aspirations.

📋 WAEC Marking Guide

  • Topic coverage: Both causes AND effects clearly discussed ✓
  • Evidence: Statistics (33%, 600,000, 100,000), specific examples
  • Logical organisation: Causes first, then effects, with clear topic sentences
  • Formal register: Objective, third-person, no contractions
  • Conclusion: Synthesises arguments, suggests solutions
English Article WAEC 2019

Essay 5: Article for a Newspaper

Question: Write an article for publication in your school magazine on "The Dangers of Drug Abuse Among Youths."

📝 Model Answer

THE SILENT EPIDEMIC: DRUG ABUSE AMONG NIGERIAN YOUTH

By Adaeze Okonkwo, SS3 Science

Walk through any Nigerian university campus, secondary school gate, or street corner in Lagos, Kano, or Port Harcourt, and you will witness an alarming phenomenon: young people, some as young as twelve, hooked on substances that are slowly destroying their futures. From codeine cough syrup and tramadol to Indian hemp and the synthetic drug commonly called "colorado," drug abuse has become a silent epidemic ravaging an entire generation.

The reasons young people turn to drugs are varied and often heartbreaking. Peer pressure tops the list — the desperate need to "belong" to a group leads many adolescents to experiment with substances they would otherwise reject. Others use drugs as an escape from poverty, academic stress, family conflict, or unemployment. Tragically, social media glamorises drug use through music videos and influencer culture, presenting addiction as cool rather than catastrophic.

The dangers of drug abuse are devastating and far-reaching. Medically, drugs damage vital organs — the liver, kidneys, lungs, and especially the brain. Long-term users develop psychiatric disorders, including schizophrenia and severe depression. Yaba Psychiatric Hospital reports that over 70 percent of recent admissions involve drug-related mental illness. Many of these young patients never fully recover.

Academically and economically, drug abuse derails ambitions. Bright students drop out, talented athletes lose opportunities, and promising careers crumble. Beyond personal loss, addicts burden their families financially and emotionally, while contributing to rising crime rates as they commit theft and violence to fund their habits.

Yet hope is not lost. Tackling this epidemic requires a coordinated response. Parents must pay closer attention to behavioural changes in their children. Schools should implement comprehensive drug-awareness programmes. Government agencies like the NDLEA must intensify enforcement against traffickers, while pharmacies should strictly regulate prescription drugs. Most importantly, churches, mosques, and community organisations should provide rehabilitation and reintegration support for victims.

The fight against drug abuse is not someone else's problem — it is yours and mine. Every young Nigerian who chooses sobriety and helps a friend do the same is a soldier in this war. Let us rise together and reclaim our generation. Our future depends on it.

📋 WAEC Marking Guide

  • Article format: Headline, byline, journalistic style ✓
  • Audience awareness: School magazine — students as readers
  • Hook opening: Vivid scene, statistics, immediate engagement
  • Persuasive call to action: Clear closing message
  • Local references: NDLEA, Yaba Hospital, named cities
English Formal Letter WAEC 2024

Essay 6: Formal Letter

Question: Write a letter to the Commissioner for Education in your state, complaining about the poor state of facilities in your school and suggesting solutions.

📝 Model Answer

Government Secondary School,
123 Independence Road,
Ikeja, Lagos State.
15th May, 2026.

The Honourable Commissioner for Education,
Lagos State Ministry of Education,
The Secretariat,
Alausa, Ikeja.

Dear Sir/Madam,

POOR STATE OF FACILITIES AT GOVERNMENT SECONDARY SCHOOL, IKEJA

I am writing on behalf of the students of Government Secondary School, Ikeja, to bring to your kind attention the deteriorating state of educational facilities in our school. As leaders entrusted with the future of Lagos State, we believe you would wish to be informed of these challenges and the steps necessary to address them.

Firstly, our classrooms are severely overcrowded, with an average of 75 students per class designed for 40. Many of us sit on broken benches or share single chairs. Several windows are without glass, exposing students to rain during the wet season and dust during harmattan. Three classroom roofs leak so badly that lessons are routinely cancelled when it rains.

Secondly, our science laboratories are practically non-functional. Chemistry apparatus dates back to the 1990s; many test tubes are broken, and reagents have long expired. Biology students have not seen a working microscope in two academic sessions, while the Physics laboratory has no functional ammeter or voltmeter. As candidates preparing for WAEC practicals, we are gravely disadvantaged.

Thirdly, the school library houses fewer than 200 books, most of which are obsolete editions. Computer Studies students share three working computers among 150 students, with no internet access. Sanitation facilities are equally inadequate — only four functional toilets serve over 1,500 students.

To remedy these challenges, we humbly suggest the following: (1) immediate renovation of leaking roofs and replacement of broken furniture; (2) re-equipping of all three science laboratories before the next academic session; (3) modernisation of the library with current textbooks and provision of adequate computer and internet facilities; and (4) construction of additional toilet blocks with proper water supply.

We are confident that under your dynamic leadership, these reasonable requests will receive prompt attention. We thank you in advance for your kind consideration.

Yours faithfully,

__________________
EMEKA OKAFOR
Senior Prefect

📋 WAEC Marking Guide

  • Format (10 marks): Both addresses, date, salutation, heading (capitals/bold), close, signature ✓
  • Salutation–Close match: "Dear Sir" with "Yours faithfully" ✓
  • Heading: Bold/capitalised summary of letter purpose ✓
  • Content: Specific complaints + concrete solutions
  • Tone: Formal, respectful, persuasive throughout
English Comprehension WAEC 2023

Essay 7: Comprehension Strategies

Approach: WAEC comprehension passages typically test inference, vocabulary in context, and main idea identification.

📝 Six-Step Comprehension Method

Step 1: Skim the passage (60 seconds). Read at speed-reading pace to grasp the general topic, tone, and structure. Do not pause for unfamiliar words.

Step 2: Read the questions before re-reading. This activates your "search lens" — you now know what to look for during your second reading.

Step 3: Read the passage carefully. Underline or mentally mark sentences that relate directly to questions. Pay attention to topic sentences (usually first sentence of each paragraph).

Step 4: Answer in your own words where possible. Direct quotation should only be used when specifically requested. WAEC examiners reward paraphrasing because it demonstrates comprehension.

Step 5: Use evidence from the passage. For inference questions ("What does the writer suggest?"), always link your answer to specific words or phrases in the text.

Step 6: Check vocabulary questions in context. "The word 'austere' as used in line 14 means..." — re-read the sentence in which the word appears, plus the sentence before and after, to deduce meaning from context clues.

Common Question Types:

• Factual: "According to the passage, what caused..." → Find direct answer
• Inference: "What can be concluded about..." → Read between the lines
• Vocabulary: "The word X means..." → Use context clues
• Main idea: "The central theme is..." → Look at title, opening, and closing
• Tone: "The author's attitude is..." → Look at adjectives and word choice

📋 Top Tips

  • Allocate 15 minutes per comprehension passage
  • Always answer in complete sentences unless told otherwise
  • Never leave a question blank — attempt every one
  • For "Yes/No" questions, always include justification
